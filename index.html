What really controls a small weir?

A small weir is easy to draw as a section. It is harder to understand as a system. Change discharge, crest length or height and the effect can travel through head, unit discharge, jump behaviour, material demand, stability and the foundation question. This article follows those links using the study’s synthetic evidence.

00

A small weir looks simple. Its dependencies are not.

This is not a search for one “best” section. It is a sequence of questions about which variables matter, under what conditions, and where the evidence becomes uncertain. Each chapter starts with the evidence, then separates interpretation from limitation.

Research boundary. This is a synthetic research article, not a design calculator or construction guide. It can clarify relationships and trade-offs, but final field design still requires site-specific hydrology, foundation evidence, applicable standards and professional engineering review.

The simple section that was not simple

The investigation began with a section that looked straightforward on paper. A closer review showed that geometry, centroid, uplift, HFL/depth assumptions and downstream protection were not all internally resolved.

The useful question therefore became broader than checking arithmetic: is the whole hydraulic–structural system being represented consistently?

02

One change travels through the whole system

A change in one input does not stay local. Discharge, crest length and coefficient determine head; head and unit discharge shape the downstream hydraulic state; geometry affects material demand and stability; and foundation class changes the scour question.

So the first research task was to separate what can vary independently from what is already fixed by an equation or dependency.

04

Head is a balance, not a single driver

The head relationship is exact within the selected broad-crested formulation: H = [Q/(C·L)]2/3. More discharge raises head, while more overflow length spreads the same discharge and lowers it. The exact elasticities are +2/3 for Q and −2/3 for L and C.

Head is only the first response. Because Q/L also sets unit discharge, the same variables immediately carry into the downstream forcing question.

05

Forcing rises faster than it first appears

Within the eligible formal-jump research domain, the fitted synthetic response follows P′ ∝ q1.67 Fr11.87 with R² ≈ 0.9999. Holding the other variable fixed, a 10% rise in q corresponds to about 17% more forcing, while a 10% rise in Fr1 corresponds to about 19.5% more.

That response is strong, but it is not a stand-alone design answer. Any attempt to reduce forcing also has to be read alongside geometry and material demand.

06

Why height quickly becomes a material penalty

The body-area proxy follows A = P·T + 0.5·s·P². Height enters the downstream batter term quadratically, so the material proxy rises nonlinearly as the section becomes taller.

But a smaller material proxy does not settle the section. Stability responds to a different combination of variables and has to be read separately.

07

Stability does not follow one simple rule

Within the applicability-conditioned diagnostic regression, cohesion has the strongest favourable coefficient and height remains adverse; downstream slope, discharge and tailwater also contribute. The pooled R² is about 0.876.

Important. These are diagnostic multivariable coefficients. They are not a universal field factor-of-safety equation and should not be read as one.

08

There is no single optimum weir

Among 2,585 eligible competent-rock cases, 101 were nondominated when material proxy, forcing, tailwater mismatch and rock threshold were minimized together. In other words, no single case could be called better across all four objectives at once. None is a scalar winner.

The useful result is therefore the shape of the trade-off, not the identity of one supposedly “best” case. Exact Pareto membership remains sample-sensitive.

10

High forcing does not automatically mean greater jump-development demand

Unit discharge can raise forcing strongly even when normalized jump-development metrics remain similar at comparable Froude number. The two responses are connected, but they answer different questions: forcing describes response magnitude, while jump-development metrics describe the demand for dissipation development.

Reference only. The jump-development lengths shown here are reference scaling relationships used in this research. They are not final IS 4997:2026 stilling-basin dimensions.

11

Scour is where the models begin to disagree

Bormann–Julien (BJ) and D’Agostino–Ferro (DAF) can both be evaluated in 14,369 common synthetic states, yet they do not tell the same story. Median DAF/BJ is ≈ 1.107, Spearman ρ is ≈ 0.422, and only about 50.3% of cases fall within a factor of two.

That disagreement has structure. The next step is to describe where and how the models diverge, not to average them into a single number.

When everything varies together, what matters most?

Global Sobol/Jansen analysis asks a different question: when the selected variables vary together across the stated synthetic ranges, which ones contribute most to each response? First-order influence is separated from total influence, including interactions. The answer depends on the response; head, forcing, jump behaviour, body area and rock threshold do not share one universal ranking.

15

What this research can — and cannot — say

What it establishes

The study shows how selected hydraulic, geometric, stability and foundation-related responses behave across the defined synthetic research domain. It identifies recurring trade-offs, shows where models disagree, and indicates which variables matter for specific responses under the stated analysis.

What remains outside the claim

It does not provide site validation or construction-ready dimensions. Final IS 4997:2026 basin provisions remain separate field-design evidence work, and the research does not establish a universal alluvial scour law or a final rock/foundation-depth method for field release.

16

Methods, data, references and author

Synthetic experiment

The synthetic experiment progressed from a baseline section through one-at-a-time tests and pairwise response surfaces to four independent 50,000-scenario LHS replications. Eligibility filtering, Pareto analysis and normalized family analysis were then applied to the relevant research populations.

Global sensitivity

A dedicated scrambled Sobol/Jansen design used base N = 8,192 on validated response surrogates. The published indices are specific to the stated synthetic ranges. Stability is intentionally excluded from unconditional Sobol ranking because its diagnostic response is applicability-conditioned.

Scour-model comparison

BJ and DAF are compared only where both models are available in the common synthetic overlap. The compact envelope describes disagreement between the two models; it is not a physical scour equation and neither model is treated as field truth.

Data provenance

Authoritative source tables remain the build inputs. The public article reads compact render-ready JSON from the study analysis. Original PNG/JPG figures are retained only as visual references and are never used as numerical data sources.
